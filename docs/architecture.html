Headcount

Volume is not consensus.

We show how much of the online volume behind a claim comes from likely automated accounts, who carried it, and how it compares with what accountable newsrooms report.

The 30-second version

When thousands of accounts repeat a claim, reporters write “people are saying”, markets move and readers believe it, and AI agents now make that volume almost free. Text Headcount a claim, or type any story. In under a minute, from public Bluesky data, we sort the posts into the narratives people are pushing, score every account behind them for automation, show what the automated accounts have in common and which real people spread them, and set it all next to what wire services and public broadcasters reported. We never rule on whether a claim is true. We measure who is pushing it.

One claim in, five answers out

You give it a claim by text or in the web checker, about the Jensen Huang week or any other story. Headcount answers with the narratives carrying the claim, the likely automated share of each with an interval and a baseline, what those accounts have in common, which real accounts amplified them, and what ten newsrooms reported. When a sample is too small to say something, the answer says so instead of guessing.

The heavy work runs once, so a known story answers in under a second

The Jensen week is pulled, labeled, scored and clustered offline, so a corpus claim is a search over saved results. Any other story takes the live path: search Bluesky, embed, group, then score every author on the spot. Embedding on CPU is the slowest step. Every answer, by text or on the web, goes through one API that writes the reply in code and scrubs private accounts.

The bot model learns from accounts that admit to being bots

It finds 8 times as many hidden bots as the obvious baseline

Nobody hands you a list of bots, so the model learns from the 287 accounts that label themselves and treats everyone else as unknown. It reads behavior only and gives each account a score with three reasons in plain words. Honest limit: self-labeled bots are mostly news and trend feeds, so that is the automation it finds best. On bot operators it never saw, it reaches 0.232, below our 0.3 bar.

No bot campaign is defending Huang.

News-relay narratives have the highest point estimates, but their intervals overlap the baseline. “Defending Huang” sits at the baseline and “Jokes and memes” is clearly below it.

The hedge survives the share button and drops out in the retelling

Spy-claim headlines and posts. The only three “smear campaign” headlines all attribute that framing to China.

No kind of real account was more likely to amplify automation

630 real accounts amplified likely automated posts; 616 (98%) were matched one-to-one on narrative, 6-hour window, reach, activity and followers.

Across the story, 4.5% of posts come from likely automated accounts. Board, market and spy-plot posts, the narratives that relay news, have the highest point estimates, but their intervals overlap the baseline, so we call that suggestive. “Jokes and memes” (0.4%) is clearly below it. “Defending Huang” is 4.8% (2.0 to 8.7%), no different from the baseline, so the Post’s “online bots defending Huang” (reported on X) doesn’t show up on Bluesky. The order of the point estimates holds at all four cutoffs, and the archetype result is a null.

Hard questions, short answers

How do you know they’re bots?

We don’t claim to. Each account gets a “likely automated” score from 39 behavior signals, shown with its top three reasons. On hidden labeled bots, the model puts 36% in its top 100 of 20,482 scored accounts; posting rate alone gets 4.5%. By hand, 46 of the 50 top-scored accounts with no bot label were automated.

Aren’t your training labels biased?

Yes, and we say so. Self-labeled bots are mostly honest news and trend feeds, so the model is best at that kind. We treat unlabeled accounts as unknown, test on bot operators the model never saw (0.232 at the top 100), and show coordination evidence separately.

Did you pick the cutoff to get the headline?

No. Every share is also shown at 0.3, 0.5, 0.7 and 0.9. Board vs jokes stays 15 to 18 times apart at every cutoff, and the order of narratives holds.

Why Bluesky and not X?

Bluesky’s full network is public and free. X charges $0.005 a post, which is over $5,000 for our pull. The method needs only posts, profiles and interactions, so it carries over to any platform that exposes them.

Who picks the newsrooms?

We did: ten wire services and public broadcasters, each labeled with its funding (for example “Qatar state-funded”). We call it “what these newsrooms report”, never the truth. It shows which narratives exist only on social media and whether careful wording survives.

So the Washington Post was wrong?

Not necessarily. They reported bots on X. On Bluesky, “Defending Huang” is 4.8% likely automated (2.0 to 8.7%), no different from the 4.5% baseline. Our model sees news-feed automation best, so campaign-style accounts would show up in the coordination evidence.

Isn’t the archetype result a failure?

It’s a clean null. After matching on narrative, time, reach, activity and followers, no group of real accounts was more likely to amplify automation. An earlier unmatched run said new small accounts were 1.32× as likely; that was the topic, not the person. One group, quote-posters, was less likely (0.46×), which we treat as exploratory.

Does removing the bots change the story?

On Bluesky, no. With likely automated accounts and their engagement removed, the narrative ranking stays the same, because automation is 4.5% of posts. The highest point estimates are in news-relay narratives (board and succession 7.7%, 5.1 to 11.0, against 4.5% overall), but those intervals overlap the baseline, so we call that suggestive.

Could bad actors use it?

It’s defensive and read-only. It never posts, never names private people, and reports aggregate shares with reasons drawn from public behavior. It adds measurement, not access.

What’s live and what’s precomputed?

For the Jensen week, the data, labels, scores, clusters and archetypes are precomputed, so a claim is a search over them (0.3 to 0.9 s). Any other story runs live: search, embed, group and score the authors in 13 to 26 s. The dashboard reads one precomputed file.

How accurate is the narrative labeling?

DeepSeek labeled every post. The fast classifier agrees with those labels 70% of the time (77% for side). Hand labels are still pending, so we don’t quote an accuracy against people.

What about images and video?

We read post text and whether a post links out. We don’t analyze images or video. The bot score rests on behavior (timing, mix, repetition), which doesn’t depend on media.

Scale and cost?

Everything runs on CPU: a small embedding model, a bot model that trains in 284 s, and one LLM call per 25 posts (about 1,600 calls for the whole corpus). A live check of 300 posts finishes in under 30 s. We haven’t measured a dollar figure for DeepSeek.

What’s next?

Hand labels for the narratives, a VM re-check of the top 50, an independently written claim set for retrieval, coordination detection aimed at campaign-style accounts, a second platform, a dedicated Photon line, and a one-page report an editor can forward.
